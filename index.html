Computational materials science · Atlas Materials Physics Lab

Simulating bio-based polyurethanes

Advisor: Dr. Tod Pascal, Department of Chemical and NanoEngineering, UC San Diego. Mentor: Robert Ramji. Experimental TPUs characterized by the Burkart Research Group. Supported by NSF through the UCSD MRSEC (DMR-2011924).

Abstract

The rising prevalence of microplastics, a class of pollutants with exponentially increasing environmental concentrations, and their associated health risks has accelerated the demand for renewable and biodegradable alternatives to petroleum-derived polymers. Polyurethanes (PUs), a versatile class of materials with applications spanning automotive components, bedding, footwear, and coatings, are formed through a step-growth polymerization reaction between diisocyanate and polyol units joined via urethane linkages. However, conventional polyurethanes are not biodegradable, and thus present great sustainability challenges. Recent advances have focused on developing polyurethane formulations derived from renewable components, increasing biodegradability.

In this work, we employ ab initio and classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to model bio-derived thermoplastic polyurethanes (TPUs) and probe their nanoscale conformations and mechanical response under tensile deformation. Model validation was achieved through in-silico production of wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) profiles and thermomechanical properties for TPUs previously formulated and characterized experimentally. Our results suggest that computational modeling can reasonably capture key structure–property relationships in bio-derived TPUs and offer valuable insights into material behavior, establishing a foundation for the development of computational design rules and a high-throughput materials discovery platform.

The problem

Polyurethanes are everywhere (shoes, car seats, mattresses) and are not biodegradable. Bio-based replacements exist, but there are few comprehensive, high-throughput computational property datasets for polymers to help screen them.

A thermoplastic polyurethane is built from hard and soft segments (plus a chain extender) joined by urethane linkages. The bio-based TPUs studied here use azelaic-acid polyester soft segments with diol chain extenders (PDO, NDO) and diisocyanates of different chain length (6-HDI, 7-HDI).

The goal

Produce WAXS profiles in silico for the TPUs the Burkart Research Group has characterized, and see whether the simulated structure matches experiment.

WAXS measures X-ray scattering at large angles. In k-space it reports how ordered a material is: a narrow peak means crystalline, a broad peak means amorphous. Peak position gives the characteristic spacing through d = 2π/k. The simulated profile is the static structure factor, S(k) = (1/N) Σᵢ Σⱼ exp(−i k·(rᵢ − rⱼ)).

Method

Each TPU starts as a SMILES string and becomes a simulation-ready polymer in five steps, using RadonPy.

  1. Build the soft segment
  2. Build the hard segment
  3. Assemble the repeat unit
  4. Build the final TPU chain
  5. Run molecular dynamics

RadonPy protocol

  • Packing
  • 21-step compression and decompression
  • 5 ns production run at 300 K

Slow-cooling protocol

  • Cool from 700 K to 300 K in 50 K steps
  • 10 ps per cooling step, then 1 ns equilibration
  • Anisotropic NPT equilibration, collect time-averaged box dimensions
  • Fix the cell at the average, then NVT equilibration
  • 5 ns production run

Results

Peak position

For TPU1 (DP30, n10) the simulated peak sits at d = 4.33 Å against 3.87 Å in experiment. The larger spacing says the simulated system is not as tightly packed.

Peak width

The simulated peak is broader than the measured one, so the simulated structure is more amorphous than the real material.

System size

Changing chain length and chain count (DP10 with 10 chains versus DP5 with 20 chains) gave nearly overlapping profiles.

Protocol

Doubling the Lennard-Jones ε, scaling RESP charges down 20%, slow-cooling, and the standard RadonPy protocol all produced nearly the same S(k).

NDO-TPU

Experiment shows a sharp peak near 1.45 Å⁻¹ with a smaller one near 1.6 Å⁻¹. The simulation puts a broad halo near 1.4 Å⁻¹ instead.

Strain

Simulated tensile strain (rate 1e-5, to 100% and 300%) left the peak position essentially unchanged.

Schematic built from the reported d-spacings and relative peak widths, not the raw scattering data.

Where it stands

I haven't yet achieved WAXS agreement. The offset in peak position and width does not move with system size or with the protocol changes above, which is why the next step is the force field.

Next

  • Implement or develop a new force field
  • Try united-atom and coarse-grained models

Longer term

  • Model other thermo-mechanical properties, such as stress–strain
  • Implement computational design rules
  • Build a high-throughput materials discovery platform

Disorder alone can stop a wave.

A numerical study of Anderson localization in one- and two-dimensional tight-binding lattices: how random on-site energy turns spreading, extended states into confined ones.

This follows the report's 1D time-evolution code: build the tridiagonal Hamiltonian (t = 1, N = 100), diagonalize it, project the site-50 starting state onto the eigenstates, multiply each by exp(−iEₙt), and sum. It runs in your browser with its own seeded random landscape, so the numbers differ slightly from the report's NumPy realization. Pause stops it, and it stops by itself at t = 40.

The model

Anderson localization, first described by P. W. Anderson in 1958, is the suppression of transport by disorder alone: no confining potential and no interactions between particles, just repeated scattering and interference. For weak disorder the wave still diffuses. As disorder grows, constructive interference of return paths enhances backscattering, transmission decays exponentially with distance, and the particle stays in a finite region.

H = −t Σ (|i⟩⟨i+1| + |i+1⟩⟨i|) + Σ εᵢ |i⟩⟨i|

The hopping strength is t = 1, and each on-site energy εᵢ is drawn uniformly from [−W/2, W/2], so W sets the disorder. To measure localization I used the inverse participation ratio, IPR(ψ) = Σᵢ |ψᵢ|⁴. It is about 1/N for a state spread evenly over N sites and reaches 1 for a state on a single site.

How I computed it

Exact diagonalization

The tight-binding Hamiltonian is a real symmetric tridiagonal matrix, diagonalized with scipy.linalg.eigh. IPR is averaged over eigenstates and many independent disorder realizations.

Time evolution

The initial state is projected onto each eigenstate and every term is multiplied by e−iEₙt (ħ = 1), which solves the time-dependent Schrödinger equation semi-analytically.

2D extension

A square lattice is mapped onto a one-dimensional index, so a 3 × 3 lattice becomes a 9 × 9 matrix, with hopping −t to all eight surrounding sites (sides and diagonals) and onsite disorder on the diagonal. The same methods then apply.

One dimension

At W = 0 every eigenstate is Bloch-like and spans the chain, with IPR near 0.015. Band-edge states localize first, because their low group velocity leaves them without the kinetic energy to cross the energy fluctuations. By W = 2 a finite localization length appears, and for W of 4 and above each eigenstate sits on a few sites.

The wave-packet dynamics tell the same story. At W = 0 the packet spreads ballistically in a light cone and the mean squared displacement exceeds 1300 sites². It drops to about 200 at W = 2, about 30 at W = 4, and just a few at W = 8, where the packet stays on a narrow stripe around its starting site.

Two dimensions

The trend matches 1D, but it is more gradual. On the 100 × 100 lattice the IPR of the plotted eigenstate is 0.0003 at W = 0 and 0.5, 0.0004 at W = 2, and 0.0056 at W = 8, where the probability collects in a small patch. Wave packets still spread across much of the lattice at W = 2, and the mean squared displacement at t = 100 falls from about 1650 (W = 0) to about 1550 (W = 2) and 1150 (W = 8), far above the 1D values.

Two-dimensional systems localize at any disorder in the infinite-size limit, but the localization length grows exponentially as disorder weakens. When it exceeds the lattice size, the states look extended, so a finite 2D lattice needs stronger disorder than a 1D chain to show pronounced localization.

GRIN miniscope: 1:1 relay for fluorescence imaging

A miniature fluorescence microscope optical system designed and optimized in Ansys Zemax OpticStudio.

As part of the Computational Imaging Systems Lab at the University of California, San Diego, I designed and optimized a miniature fluorescence microscope (miniscope) built using multiple GRIN (gradient-index) rod lenses. Inspired by the "Krill-Eye" Optical System proposed by researchers at the MIT Media Laboratory [1], the lenses are cascaded into a superposition compound system, and the layout is designed to accommodate an emission bandpass filter and an external illumination system. The goal is to achieve 1:1 magnification and gapless imaging in order to produce properly superimposed images and introduce more symmetry. We aim to maximize the numerical aperture and the usable field of view while minimizing Seidel aberrations.

Why GRIN lenses

GRIN rod lenses bend light with a smoothly varying, parabolic refractive-index profile. That internal symmetry corrects spherical aberration, and 1:1 gapless imaging is much easier to achieve with GRIN lenses. They are also relatively cheap, extremely compact, and easy to mount and stack, a natural fit for a miniscope where size and weight are tightly constrained.

A rod's length is measured in pitch (P), the fraction of one full sinusoidal ray period. A 0.25P rod collimates light from a point on its face. A 0.5P rod forms an inverted 1:1 image, which is what each rod in this relay does.

System architecture

  • Cascaded GRIN relay. Several GRIN rod lenses are arranged as a superposition compound system. Each set of the four GRIN lenses as shown above produces a 1:1 image of the same object point at the same location on the image plane. Superposing these images gathers more light than a single narrow rod and increases the usable field of view, while the rods’ internal symmetry keeps spherical aberration, distortion, coma, and lateral color low.
  • External illumination. The design leaves room for a bandpass filter for the emitted fluorescence between the lenses. To keep the system compact, an external illumination system will be used, which inherently separates the excitation and emission light and removes the need for a dichroic mirror or beamsplitter.
  • Two-field model. The system is evaluated on-axis and at a 1 mm object height, which shows how performance degrades toward the edge of the field.

Design goals and optimization

The main trade-off in this design is field of view and numerical aperture versus aberration. A wider field is more useful for imaging, and numerical aperture helps with resolution and light collection, but off-axis rays push the Seidel aberrations (spherical aberration, coma, astigmatism, field curvature, and distortion) up quickly. My workflow:

  • Set up the prescription in sequential mode with the GRIN rod lenses, and trace both fields at 0.530 µm.
  • Define merit function targets for unit magnification and a small spot at the image plane.
  • Iterate on lens spacing and focus positions, and test how small shifts of individual surfaces affect the on-axis and off-axis spots.
  • Check the front of the first lens for vignetting. A few marginal rays were clipped there, so I worked on trimming them out of the beam.

Results so far

0.238 µmRMS spot radius, on-axis
12.84 µmRMS spot radius, 1 mm field
0.996 mmimage height for a 1.000 mm object

On-axis performance is essentially diffraction-limited, and the image height is within half a percent of the object height, so the relay is very close to true 1:1. The off-axis field is the weak point. Its spot is larger and elliptical, which points to residual astigmatism and field aberrations, and that is where my current optimization effort is focused.

Next steps

  • Reduce the off-axis spot size while holding the on-axis performance.
  • Design the external illumination system.
  • Physically build a prototype.
